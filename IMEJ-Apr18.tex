\begin{abstract}
Recent developments in self-reacting oscillating Wave Energy Convertors (WECs) point toward two popular design concepts. These designs are either semi-submerged system in which a surface piercing float reacts against a relatively still heave plate or a single body system that reacts against the ocean floor.  The first design employs a rigid spar connection between the actuator and the reaction body; however, the second design uses a flexible belt connection between the actuator and the foundation. In this work, a two-body sub-surface WEC is proposed in which the submerged float reacts against a base that is dynamically active. The dynamics of the reacting body is influenced by both the buoyancy parameters and the PTO generated tether tension. As a result, the two-body system oscillates in an antipodal mode. The optimal hydrostatic conditions that result in a design that can generate mechanical power, in single degree of freedom motion, under the wave excitation are discussed in this work. The hydrodynamic analysis is carried out for different wave conditions observed off the coast of US. Optimal PTO parameters are determined for various designs. The analysis is carried out using Ansys-Aqwa. Observations of the relative predictive performance between the two numerical simulations models are delineated.\\
\color{red}{Currently 204 words limit is 250}
\end{abstract}

\IMEJPARstart{D}{ue} to the concerted effort to achieve energy independence as well as to reduce the greenhouse gas emission, most of the development to date in offshore renewable energy systems has been targeted at “utility-scale” or “community-scale” devices. These devices have electricity generation capacities in the order of $\mathrm{MW}$ and $\mathrm{kW}$ respectively. However, another application area that is attracting investment in wave energy converters is “small-scale” devices. These devices can provide autonomous power to various ocean-bound systems, such as autonomous underwater vehicles~\cite{chen2024unified}, observation systems~\cite{doe2018wind}, and even aquaculture farms. The expectation is that these devices can be used to power the on-site stand-alone systems which are located remotely, resulting in exponential growth in the sector often referred as “Blue economy”\cite{green2019enabling,cavagnaro2020powering}.
This paper provides an overview of the initial design process of such a device. First, the operating principle and primary dimension of the device is derived and discussed in~\secref{sec:device_details}~based on the constraints of the operating conditions. The numerical modelling approach is described in~\secref{sec:numerical_modelling}. The details of the deployment sites are discussed in~\secref{sec:deployment_sites}. The results and the following discussion are presented in~\secref{sec:results_discussion}.

\section{Background}
Numerical benchmarking of eight WECs with different working principles was conducted to evaluate their power matrices and site specific annual energy production~\cite{babarit2012}. Out of these, two operated close to sea-bed and only one was near surface submerged WECs. This shows that the focus has mainly been around floating devices. Some of the popular floating WECs with heave plate are MBARI-WEC~\cite{hamiltonMBARIWECPowerSource2021}, OPT Powerbuoy, OSU-Lupa (Laboratory Upgrade Point Absorber)~\cite{beringer_numerical_2026} and Wavebob~\cite{beatty_wave_2019}. 


Though the wave energy decays exponentially with depth in deep water regime, subsurface WECs provide the potential benefit of survivability and covertness. Possibly these are reasons why subsurface WECs are emerging as a preferred design choice for WEC developers. Some of the popular designs that are either bottom fixed or do not have a heave plate as reacting bodies are AWS Ocean Energy’s Archimedes Waveswing, Symphony Wave Power’s WEC, Carnegie Clean Energy’s CETO 6~\cite{sergiienkoOptimalArrangementMooring2016}, and CalWave’s xWave. CETO6 and xWave are multi tethered, multi DOF point absorbers which operate close to the surface. The Waveswing is an air pressure differential point absorber tethered to the sea bed and Symphony Wave Power’s WEC is a bottom-fixed device operating in shallow waters.
Conceptual submerged WEC capable of producing $10~\mathrm{W}$ of power with a float and six trubines has been proposed in~\cite{jmse12010137}.
\par In all the buoyant designs mentioned above the buoyancy of the float acts as the actuation force and either the foundation in the sea bed or the heave plate acts as a reaction body. In this work we propose a two-body submerged system with a float and heave plate connected by a tether and the entire system is moored to the foundation. The float will act as an actuator and the buoyancy of the heave plate will aid to power generation. This work will evaluate the performance for the above described WEC and compare six different design candidates at different wave energy sites in the US. 

\section{Device Details}
\label{sec:device_details}
The WEC design under study is a two-body point absorber consisting of a submerged float and a heave plate. The design of the WEC supports the sub-surface operation.~The sketch of primary components used in the numerical modelling are shown in~\figref{fig_QP}. The float and heave plate are connected by a tether which is attached to the top of the heave plate and the bottom of the float. The heave plate is moored to the sea bed using a mooring line. The mooring line is attached to the bottom of the heave plate and the other end is attached to a foundation on the sea bed. The tether and mooring line are assumed to be linear elastic with a stiffness of $k$ and $0.1k$ respectively. The tether and mooring line are assumed to be massless and inextensible.

\section{Numerical Modelling}
\label{sec:numerical_modelling}
Commercially available software~\enquote{Ansys-Aqwa\textregistered{}} was used to both calculate the frequency dependent parameters and to simulate the time-dependent dynamic behavior of the WEC.
\subsection{Cable modelling}
A linear elastic cable module in Aqwa is the simplest numerical model of an actual mooring line. Aqwa requires the user to specify the unstretched length instead of pretension for elastic tethers. With the knowledge of stiffness and pretension (calculated as the difference between the buoyancy and the weight of the float and Heave Plate), the stretched length can be determined.
\begin{align}
L^{t} &= \frac{(V_f-V_f^{disp})\rho_w g}{k} \label{eq:tether_stretched} \\
L_0^{t} &= D_{hp}-(H_f+D_f)-L^{t} \label{eq:tether_unstretched} \\
L^{m} &= \frac{(V_f+V_{hp})-(V_f^{disp}+V_{hp}^{disp})\rho_w g}{0.1k} \label{eq:mooring_stretched} \\
L_0^{m} &= D-(D_{hp}+H_{hp})-L^{m} \label{eq:mooring_unstretched}
\end{align}
The stretched length $L^{t}$ of the tether and mooring can be calculated using~\eqref{eq:tether_stretched} and~\eqref{eq:mooring_stretched}. The initial unstretched length $L_0$ of the tether and mooring can be calculated using~\eqref{eq:tether_unstretched} and~\eqref{eq:mooring_unstretched} respectively. $V_x^{disp}$ is the displaced volume of the respective cylindrical geometry ($x$ can be either~\enquote{$f$} for float or~\enquote{$hp$} for heave plate.), $\rho_w$ is the density of water, $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity, $k$ is the stiffness of the tether, $D_{x}$ is the distance from the free surface to the respective cylindrical geometry, $H_x$ is the height of the respective cylindrical geometry.~$D$ is the depth of water at deployment location~(considered $=70~\mathrm{m}$ for all the simulations).

\subsection{PTO algorithm}
The PTO system is modelled as a linear spring-damper system. The total force acting on the PTO system is the sum of the mechanical spring force and the generator force.
This force is given by~\ref{eq:mech_force}
\begin{equation}
F_{PTO} =
\begin{cases}
C + K_{mech}\Delta L^t + B \dot{\Delta L^t}, & \text{if } L^t > L_0^t \\
0, & \text{if } L^t \leq L_0^t
\end{cases}
\label{eq:mech_force}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the constant pretension, $K_{mech}$ is the spring stiffness, \(\Delta L^t =(L^t - L_0^t)\) is the tether extension and $B$ is the damping coefficient.

In this project three PTO models have been developed. These models are
% \begin{enumerate}
% \item Aqwa (Elastic Cable-Fender) model
% \item UDF (Spring-Damper) model
% \item UDF with limiting (Spring-Damper) model
% \end{enumerate}
% The first model is the default Aqwa model which uses a linear elastic cable to model the PTO. The second model is a User Defined Function (UDF) that models the PTO as a linear spring-damper system. The third model is a UDF that models the PTO as a linear spring-damper system with a limiting force. The details of the UDF models are discussed in the following sections.
\subsubsection{Aqwa (Elastic Cable-Fender)}
In this model, the PTO is modelled as a combination of a linear elastic cable and a fender.
The spring stiffness is modeled using the cable element. A fender in Aqwa can have nonlinear stiffness, friction and damping. However, for this modelling only the damping properties of the fender were considered. Just like the cable element, the fender acts only in extension between a point on float and the heave plate.
\subsubsection{UDF (Spring-Damper)}
A User Defined Function (UDF) is an external program that can be called from Aqwa and coupled with the solver to enhance the existing functionality. In this work the UDF is utilized to replace the numerical PTO modeling component of Aqwa discussed above. This architecture allows us to implement arbitrary force models like providing limits and providing component characteristics that cannot be defined in standard Aqwa input files.

\section{Wave Climate at deployment locations}
\label{sec:deployment_sites}
Based on the works of~\cite{alday2024effects} on wave resource assessment off the coast of US North West, a set of guidelines were developed and included in a technical specification for wave resource assessment by International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC)~\cite{iec62600_101_2024}. The IEC brings together international marine energy experts to develop consensus-based standards that guide the development of a broad range of sectors. The current industry consensus is determination of representative sea states based on the energy weighted joint probability distribution function of primarily \(H_{s}\) and \(T_{e}/T_{P}\)~\cite{robertson2021holistic}.
However, the true description of a sea state should involve additional parameters such as spectral shape, peak direction, directional spreading, wind, tide, and current; yet data to fully characterize these aspects are often unavailable. Even when such data exist, current approaches neither develop a multidimensional probabilistic description of the deployment site nor account for energy-weighted probabilities, and they typically preclude the evaluation of WEC performance across multiple deployment climates. A need for developing a methodology that could account for multiple deployment locations was presented in a Wave Energy Prize competition by US Department of Energy in 2016. A new methodology was proposed in this competition by~\cite{bull2017wave} that applied a cluster analysis to scatter diagrams in order to determine a subset of sea states that could be scaled to find the average annual power flux at each wave climate. A common centroid representing of \(H_{s}\) and \(T_{P}\) was determined for the clusters. This procedure opens the possibility of using a single set of cluster centroid for different locations. Since WEC performance assessment simulations are computationally resource intensive, in this work the methodology described above is used to run simulations for a single set of cluster centroids and the power produced for each centroid is scaled depending on the site under consideration.
\par
A set of 18 sea states, shown in~\figref{fig_18seas}, was used throughout the analysis in this report. Wave elevations were generated using the Bretschneider spectrum. Although these wave datasets do not all correspond to the same water depth (ranges from \(42~\mathrm{m}\) to \(149~\mathrm{m}\)), they provide a useful indication of WEC performance across varied wave profiles, since the same set of sea states is applied at each location shown in~\figref{fig_location}. Note that when the weights associated with the $18$ sea states are used, they account for varying percentages of the total joint probability distribution (\(67\%-95\%\)), depending on location. This is because, at some locations, a significant portion of the year falls into sea states not represented within the \(18\) sea states considered.

Occurrence data reported by NDBC buoys were used to calculate the annual average power at that location. A cluster analysis was applied to wave scatter diagrams to identify a representative subset of sea states that can be scaled to estimate the average annual power (AAP) flux. The subset is determined by minimizing the distance between each data point and its corresponding cluster centroid. Four pdist2 options for minimizing the distance function were compared these are Squared Euclidean, Standardized Euclidean, Eclidean and Mahalanobis.  This clustering is performed using the \(k\)-means algorithm implemented in the MATLAB Statistics Toolbox, where the number of clusters and the centroid values is specified by the user. The algorithm employs an iterative partitioning procedure that optimizes the associated memberships by minimizing the sum of point-to-centroid distances across all $k$ clusters. As a result, each sea state is assigned to the cluster whose distance to centroid yields the minimum value, providing an optimally representative classification of the wave climate. In this paper results corresponding to~\enquote{Mahalanobis} method are presented.

\subsection{Variation of mass ratio}
In this sensitivity test the mass of the float and heave plate was varied to study the effect of ratio of mass \( \mathrm{MR} = \mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{F}} / \mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{HP}} \) on the average power absorbed by the wave energy converter for a PTO configuration with stiffness \(k=3000~\mathrm{N/m}~\text{and~damping}~C=2000~\mathrm{Ns/m}\). The variation of the power with \(H_s\) and \(T_p\) is shown in the~\figref{fig_massRatio}. The power increases almost linearly with significant wave height, with progressively steeper slopes at lower mass ratios. At every wave height, the ordering of the curves remains unchanged, indicating that increasing the float-to-heave plate mass ratio reduces the energy captured.
Similarly, the right subplot shows that the average power decreases approximately linearly as the peak period increases from \(7.5~\mathrm{s}~\text{to}~13.5~\mathrm{s}\). The reduction is substantial across all mass ratios, highlighting that the selected PTO configuration is better tuned to shorter-period waves. The separation between the curves persists throughout the period range, indicating that the effect of mass ratio is largely independent of wave period.
This study demonstrates mass ratio acts as an important design parameter that consistently shifts the power response, with lower mass ratios providing superior performance under all examined sea states. These results suggest that reducing the float-to-heave plate mass ratio (lighter float relative to the heave plate) improves the dynamic response of the system, allows the system to respond more effectively to wave excitation and enhances energy absorption without altering the overall dependence on the incident wave conditions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item In PTO 1 the absolute power obtained by using a set of PTO coefficients at a particular sea state at that site is multiplied by the occurrence of that sea state at that site. This method is repeated for all the other PTO coefficients. The sum of the maximum power obtained by using a constant PTO is added to obtain the annual avg power.

\end{itemize}

The power obtained with D0 is also higher, compared to any other design. However, due to high tension in the tether, this design might incur higher material cost. \section{Discussion and Conclusion}
This study investigated the hydrodynamic performance and design optimization of a submerged wave energy converter (WEC) equipped with a tether–PTO system. The report first introduced the overall system architecture, including the prime movers, tether specifications, PTO algorithm, and PTO parameters. A numerical modelling framework was then developed, covering the governing theory, model implementation, verification of the developed UDF and the selection of representative sea states for performance assessment. The mechanical average annual power capture at six different sites along the US coasts for different designs is compared.
\par
A comprehensive sensitivity analysis was conducted under both survival and operational sea states. The operational-state analysis examined the influence of key design parameters for six different designs of float and heave plate. These designs vary in characteristic dimensions. Several sensitivity analyses were carried out for different operating parameters, primarily the PTO coefficients, draft, initial tether length, mass ratio between float and heave plate and mass of heave plate. The results demonstrate that the hydrodynamic response and power capture of the submerged WEC are strongly influenced by both the system configuration and the PTO parameters. The sensitivity analyses showed that careful selection of key design parameters, including the geometric configuration, mass distribution, and the initial float–heave plate gap, can substantially enhance energy capture while maintaining acceptable structural loads and system stability. For a configuration that is submerged 2 m below the surface, the power capture at most of the sites for most of the PTO coefficients is more than the minimum targeted value (only location where power was lower was in San Diego). The variation of power capture at various depths and different tether lengths is also compared for two designs. A linear drop in the captured power is observed with an increase in the draft and increase in the initial tether length (over the range of 2-7 m submerged). The optimal PTO coefficient at six different sites around US were found out for maximum power capture.
\par
Overall, the validated numerical model and the comprehensive parametric analyses presented in this study provide a robust framework for optimizing submerged WEC designs, enabling an effective balance between energy production, structural integrity, operational reliability and survivability for site-specific deployment.
